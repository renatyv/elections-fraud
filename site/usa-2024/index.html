! Needs a closer look

Ohio Senate: what looked unusual?

The second digit in Republican vote counts appeared in an unusual pattern. The second digit of 1,234 is 2. Benford’s law gives one expected pattern for these digits, although it does not fit every dataset. I compared how often digits 0–9 appeared across 8,798 local Republican vote totals with that pattern. I also used a second model fitted to the sizes of Ohio’s vote totals.

Ohio stood out in both comparisons, even after allowing for the many tests in this study. It was the only 2024 case to do so. Seven cases from older elections also stood out in both comparisons.

!See Ohio’s actual digit pattern

Blue bars show Ohio’s results. Each bar shows how often a second digit appeared among the 8,798 Republican vote totals. The dark line shows the Benford reference pattern. The amber markers show the range usually produced by a model fitted to the sizes of Ohio’s vote totals.

What stands out: about 10% of Ohio’s totals have 0 as the second digit, compared with about 12% in the Benford pattern. Digits 7, 8 and 9 appear more often than that pattern predicts. Overall, Ohio’s bars are flatter than the reference line.

A gap between a bar and a reference shows a difference from that model. It does not show how many votes were wrong. The overall finding considers the pattern across all ten digits.

What does that tell me?

It gives me a reason to look more closely at the records. It does not tell me that votes were added, removed or switched. The two comparisons use the same vote counts, so they are not two independent pieces of evidence.

The models do not fully account for differences in reporting-area size or voter makeup. Those differences could help explain the pattern. This check cannot tell me how many votes might be affected or whether the winner would change.

What would help explain it?

A useful next step would be to check the original Ohio records, see which areas contribute most to the pattern, and repeat the comparison using areas of similar size and voting habits. Paper-ballot checks would provide evidence that digit patterns alone cannot.

!See the Ohio test results and source limits

I tested the second digits of 8,798 Republican Senate vote totals, each at least 10. Against Benford’s expected pattern, p = 9.44 × 10−14 and adjusted q = 2.18 × 10−11. Against the fitted lognormal model, using 20,000 simulations, p = 1/20,001 (about 0.00005) and adjusted q = 0.00496. A p-value describes how unusual a result is under the chosen model. The q-value also allows for the many tests run. Both comparisons remain below q < 0.05 after separate adjustments across all 694 digit tests. Neither value is the probability that fraud occurred.

I used an archived copy of the Ohio data from a separate repository. I did not download it directly from the state for this analysis. It is missing 640 Senate write-in votes compared with the House Clerk’s totals, but it includes the named Republican candidate’s counts used here. The models also leave out some differences between reporting areas.

What did the number checks find?

The clearest lead was in Ohio. The other checks did not establish fraud. Normal differences between communities can also produce unusual-looking results, and these tests can miss problems.

iSee the overview chart and test totals

110 samples from 2024 races · 65 samples from earlier races · 694 digit tests.

Ten digit tests found unusual patterns against the standard expected distributions. Eight did so against a lognormal model fitted to the vote-count sizes, and all eight also stood out in the first comparison. These results allow for the number of tests run, using the Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment separately for each comparison. Only one of the eight cases is from 2024. Neither model captures every difference in area size or voter makeup.

i

How many votes came from very high-turnout areas?

Those votes alone do not tell me whether anything went wrong.

For each sample, I set a high-turnout cutoff at its own 99.5th percentile, weighting areas by registered voters. Only 0.5% of that weighted distribution lies above the cutoff. In the 2024 samples, the areas above it contain 0–1.489% of votes for the leading party group. I have not established what the turnout curve would look like without manipulation, so I cannot turn this into a count of fake votes or a corrected result. Earlier elections provide context; they do not compare exactly the same precincts.

i

Does one party do better where turnout is higher?

The pattern changes from state to state. Differences between communities can explain it.

For each race, I measured the link between turnout and party support, giving areas different weights. I also measured the link after removing each county’s average. These correlation values are available to download. The direction of the link changes between states and follows geography, so it does not support a nationwide fraud claim. Support means candidate votes divided by all ballots, including ballots with no vote in that race.

i

Do reporting areas fall into different groups?

They do, but different voting patterns are not evidence of fraud.

For each race with usable data, a model fits two bell-shaped groups to the chart. It describes differences between reporting areas. It does not model how fraud would add or change votes, as the original research method does. The share assigned to either group is not a fraud rate.

✓

Were there too many round percentages?

No warning remained after allowing for the number of tests I ran.

I checked 525 measures, using 1,000 binomial simulations for each: made-up counts generated under the chance model. I looked for too many whole percentages and too many multiples of five, giving 1050 comparisons in all. After allowing for all those comparisons, 0 measures raised a warning under the bell-curve (normal) approximation used here. That approximation can be unreliable when unusual outcomes are rare, and the simulations use probabilities estimated from the same data. The largest results in the table are starting points for further checks.

!

Did the digits in vote counts look unusual?

Ohio’s Republican Senate counts stood out against two expected patterns. This needs a closer look.

I ran 694 digit tests. Second-digit tests use vote totals ≥10 and last-digit tests use totals ≥100; each test needs ≥100 usable totals. After separate Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) adjustments for the number of tests, 10 tests stood out against the standard patterns and 8 against the fitted lognormal model; 8 stood out in both. The first model check used 400 simulations. I then repeated 12 tests with 20,000 simulations: every standard-pattern BH flag and every result at the smallest p-value the first simulation could measure. The finer check can reach p=1/20,001. Before applying BH again across the full set, I assigned p=1 to tests not selected for the finer check. This avoids treating a coarse simulation limit as evidence that no pattern exists. Neither model captures all differences between election areas.

i

Which places looked different from the others?

The checks give me places to review, not a list of fraudulent results.

Comparing areas with their county’s typical result flagged 2,753 area-and-race records across current and earlier elections. This uses the median and median absolute deviation (MAD), a measure of spread. A separate computer method, Isolation Forest, flagged 1,451 records. Areas in the same county are not necessarily neighbours or socially similar. The computer method is set to select 1% as unusual; it was not trained on known fraud cases.

i

Did some places report exactly the same numbers?

Yes. Small reporting areas can match by chance.

In 1,673 rows, all five totals repeat another row: registered voters, ballots, Democratic votes, Republican votes and other votes. Small precincts can match by chance. To judge whether this is unusually common, I would need a chance model suited to these areas. Split precincts and overlapping Washington sources are not counted as separate independent precincts.

!

Can these checks miss a problem?

Yes. A made-up test case went undetected, so a clean result is no guarantee.

I added 45,950 made-up Republican votes to a copy of the Minnesota Senate data. Keeping the original turnout cutoff at 92.56%, Republican votes above it rose from 9,401 to 156,258. This includes genuine votes in areas pushed above the cutoff, so the increase does not measure the number added. In another test, I altered 194 areas to produce round turnout percentages. The z score, which measures distance from the expected result, rose from -1.49 to 0.31 and raised no warning. That simulated problem went undetected. These examples show limits on detection; they do not let me estimate a fraud rate.

What did Minnesota’s hand check find?

Every check passed the state’s rules. This gives me evidence from actual paper ballots for the places reviewed, but it cannot tell me about places that were not checked.

✓See the hand-check details and official report

The official 438-page report contains 657 tables, each covering one race in one of 219 precincts. Before the report’s adjustments, 115 tables show a difference between totals; afterward, 24 do. Adding the differences across candidate and ballot categories gives 365 before adjustment and 28 afterward. These sums do not count unique disputed ballots. The official label ACCEPTABLE means a check met the state’s allowed tolerance, even if the totals were not identical.

I extracted each table’s status and totals and checked the arithmetic. The Luna model separately checked four tables on three page images; it did not visually review all 438 pages. The hand count gives paper-ballot evidence for the sample reviewed. It says nothing directly about precincts outside that sample. Review summary (JSON) · Every review table (CSV).

How much of the country did I check?

I used 47,236 local reporting areas, including precincts, groups of precincts and central vote-counting pools. This is only part of the country. Not every area had enough usable data for every test.

The study focuses on Senate and House races. Older elections help put the findings in context, but changes in boundaries and voting rules make direct comparisons difficult.

Records left out and why: Some House precincts are split between districts. I left out those fragments when turnout for the whole precinct could not be assigned to them reliably. Could vote splitting change the winner?

In ten races, the votes for candidates outside the top two were larger than the gap between first and second place.

For example, Iowa’s 1st House district was decided by 799 votes. There were 967 write-in votes. If 800 of those voters had backed the runner-up instead, with the rest sitting out, the result would have flipped. I do not know if they would have made that choice.

Let M be the winner’s lead and O the votes for all other candidates. When O > M, those votes are numerous enough to change the winner on paper. If M+1 of those voters chose the runner-up and the rest sat out, the runner-up would win. If every remaining voter in that group backed the leader, the number needed is floor((O+M)/2)+1: half of O+M, rounded down, plus one. Neither scenario predicts their choices. Iowa House 1’s 967 other votes are all write-ins combined, not support for one candidate or a known group of like-minded voters.

I cannot say a candidate “spoiled” a race. The totals show what is possible on paper. They do not tell me anyone’s second choice.

What this study cannot conclude

These checks cannot tell me how many votes, if any, were fraudulent. They also cannot prove that every place without a warning sign was counted correctly.

Some records were missing or could not be compared reliably. I did not examine ballot handling or how results changed on election night. Minnesota’s hand check is the only paper-ballot review included here.

iRead the data gaps and limits of the study
  • The MEDSL totals do not all match the comparison sources. MIT Election Data + Science Lab (MEDSL) provides a separate statewide Senate table for comparison. It is not an independent certification. Differences can come from names, write-ins, hidden counts, updates and missing records. Comparing race totals with the House Clerk’s figures gives matches for 387 MEDSL races; 101 winning margins differ. Even matching race totals would not verify every precinct record.
  • The earlier MEDSL checks leave out seven states. Indiana’s data have a documented overcount involving straight-ticket votes. New Jersey combines results for different sizes of area, and Louisiana lacks precinct-level early votes. Alaska and Maine use ranked-choice voting, making first-round counts harder to compare. Oregon has unnamed candidates and rows containing ballot totals; Rhode Island has House records with no district assignment. I kept the original data for all seven states.
  • Turnout coverage is narrower. I only matched Election Assistance Commission (EAC) records that cover a whole county. A town or city code does not count as a county code. County House totals can combine several district races, and races with only one candidate can distort party comparisons.
  • I did not examine ballot handling or election-night changes. Final totals do not include ballot serial numbers, records of who handled ballots, or updates throughout election night. Minnesota’s hand-review report gives paper-ballot evidence for the 219 precincts reviewed.
  • I could not test voters’ second choices or alternative district maps. I could not download Alaska’s and Maine’s ranked-choice records for this analysis. I did not collect the boundaries and population data needed to test maps drawn to favour a party.

Because of these gaps, I cannot estimate a reliable fraud rate, establish a paradox involving voters’ preferences, or verify that every area without a warning was counted correctly.
