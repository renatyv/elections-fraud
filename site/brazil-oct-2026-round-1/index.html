What happened on 4 October 2026

Brazil held the first round of its presidential election. Voting is compulsory and ballots are cast on electronic machines (urnas eletrônicas); the electoral authority TSE publishes final results per section — 499,248 of them, each covering a few hundred voters. Nobody crossed 50%, so the two leaders meet again in a runoff.

What if the candidates or voting rules were different?

I compared totals for areas, rather than following individual voters. Similar results in the same places do not tell me what each person wanted, whether they voted tactically, or whether a voting paradox occurred. The what-if results depend on how I assume voters would react.

1

Adding votes does not create a coalition

The five candidates won 65.2 million votes (54.6%) between them. The leader won 47.0%. Adding the five totals does not show that one candidate replacing all five would win.

2

Estimating where votes went between the 2022 rounds

I used totals from 472,075 sections found in both 2022 rounds to estimate how votes moved between candidates. I did not follow individual voters. A model can match section totals well and still get individual choices wrong.

3

The same party gets different totals in different races

Caiado won 0.28 times as many presidential votes as PSD won for deputy; Zema won 0.11 times NOVO’s deputy total. These totals do not show who chose different parties for the two offices, or why.

Scenario 1 · Sum five candidates’ votes

I grouped these five candidates because they did well in similar places. Together they won 54.62% of valid votes. A single joint candidate might change who votes and whom they choose. This sum cannot show that the candidates were interchangeable or that a runoff was unnecessary.

In this made-up rule, the winner in each territory takes all its weight, and that weight depends on registered voters. Flávio Bolsonaro gets 65.9% of this constructed electoral college, compared with 51.0% of votes cast for the two leaders. I only recounted existing votes; I did not predict how people would vote under different rules.

Model · Where might the 2022 votes have gone?

I built the model using 472,075 matching sections and tested it on sections left out of the fitting. The fit score was R² = 0.976. It put Lula’s total share 0.7 percentage points above the actual result in that test sample. This checks predictions of area totals in 2022; it does not verify individual choices or show that the model works in 2026.

For Tebet’s 2022 votes, the model estimates 49.9% going to Jair Bolsonaro, 27.9% to Lula and 22.2% to blank or invalid ballots. Those are estimates based on assumptions, rather than observed choices by the same voters.

Scenario 2 · Apply the 2022 model to 2026

For this scenario, I paired 2026 candidates with 2022 candidates who did well in similar places, then reused the 2022 vote-transfer rates. That gives Flávio Bolsonaro 52.6% and Lula 47.4% of their combined vote. Pairing Flávio with Jair Bolsonaro is an assumption about two different candidates; I cannot show that they had the same voters. This is a what-if calculation, with no established forecast accuracy.

The 95% bootstrap range comes from repeatedly resampling the data used to fit the transfer model. It leaves out uncertainty about candidate pairings, changes in turnout and voter choices, and errors in how the model works. The 0.7 percentage-point error in the 2022 check is not a limit on how wrong a 2026 forecast could be.

What the checks found

I ran eleven groups of checks on section totals. Together they give no clear evidence of manipulation. Some number patterns differ from the test’s expected pattern, but the test’s assumptions may not fit these data. These checks cannot guarantee an election was free of fraud.

i
How many votes were in very high-turnout sections?

I set the high-turnout cutoff at 91.6%: the 99.5th percentile, meaning only about 0.5% of sections have higher turnout. Those sections hold 0.62% of all ballots, 0.49% of Flávio Bolsonaro’s votes and 0.80% of Lula’s. This counts votes above a chosen line; it cannot identify fake votes or set a limit on their number. Choosing a percentile ensures that only a small share of sections lies above it.

Does a candidate do better where turnout is higher? (“comet tail”)

Across Brazil, higher turnout has little link with either candidate’s vote share: the weighted correlation is −0.03 for Flávio Bolsonaro and +0.05 for Lula. Adding fake votes can sometimes create a rising tail on this chart. A link would not prove fraud, and a value near zero does not rule it out. Geography and the way areas are combined can also change the pattern.

Last digit (Beber–Scacco)

The final digits appear about equally often in the full dataset. For counts of at least 100, the largest gap from equal frequency is 0.65 percentage points. Digits 0 and 5 have at most 0.12 percentage points of extra frequency. Filtering by count size changes what pattern to expect, and evenly spread digits cannot guarantee genuine results.

Does turnout track support within each state? (Kobak et al.)

The link between turnout and Flávio Bolsonaro’s vote share varies by state: correlations run from −0.30 to +0.49. The two leaders can show opposite patterns simply because a larger share for one leaves less for the other. These links alone cannot tell ordinary voter mobilisation from manipulation.

One pair of sections has exactly the same results. Each finding needs a closer look at local circumstances. A flagged section is not necessarily manipulated, and an unflagged one is not necessarily sound.

Would the checks notice deliberately changed data?

In a test copy of the data, I added about 2 million made-up ballots using the stated recipe. The high-turnout measure grew 22-fold. Rounding turnout to whole percentages in 5% of sections raised the z score, a measure of departure from the expected pattern, from 0.35 to 9.9. The tools detected these specific changes; this does not show they would detect every manipulation of a similar size.

What this analysis cannot tell us

I checked published totals, rather than how voting machines or ballot handling worked. I used 2022 as a comparison, without assuming it was free of fraud. A test can miss manipulation. Its assumptions may not fit; nearby sections can resemble each other; and running many tests makes chance flags more likely. Matching totals for areas does not reveal individual voters’ choices.

Data and how to repeat the analysis

  • Source: official section-level (seção) results from the TSE open-data portal (resultados-2026), final 1st-round files generated 5–6 October 2026: 499,248 sections including abroad voting — about 23 GB of CSV.
  • The files agree in every check I ran: candidate votes match the detail files, blank and invalid counts match, section identifiers are unique, and turnout never exceeds registered voters. The national total matches the official result exactly. For comparison, I used both rounds of TSE resultados-2022, matching the same sections (92.4% coverage).
  • The analysis uses open-source Python tools (pandas / scikit-learn). They run the Shpilkin, Klimek, Kobak and Beber–Scacco checks, compare sections with their neighbours, use Isolation Forest to flag unusual results, compare machine models and 2022 results, and test deliberately altered data.
  • Full technical report (in Russian), the section dataset, scripts and all 18 figures / 21 tables are available in the project repository.
  • Geographic grouping, modelled 2022 transfers and conditional 2026 scenarios: see PARADOXES.md. Interpret its outputs with the limitations stated above.
