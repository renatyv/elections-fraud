Do the numbers add up?

!

No clear sign of added ballots, but two findings need a closer look

Yotova received 57.566% of the votes in the published result; Gyurov received 39.483%. The totals from all 12,717 polling stations match that result exactly. I checked voting patterns in 10,656 stations inside Bulgaria. The checks do not show a clear pattern of ballot stuffing—adding votes that people did not cast. But 14 station records contain numbers that do not balance, and some digits appear unusually often in Yotova’s vote counts. These deserve checking; they do not prove fraud.

Yotova leads in 28 of Bulgaria’s 31 districts. Kardzhali is the biggest change: she led there in round 1, but Gyurov leads in the runoff. Sofia-25 and Plovdiv-city move the other way, to Yotova. In the stations used for the turnout charts, voting rises from about 21 in every 100 registered voters to 27 in every 100.

i
What I found

The totals match. Most checks raise no alarm. Two findings need follow-up.

1

The national total adds up, but 14 station records do not balance

I added up the results from all 12,717 polling stations. Each candidate’s total matches the national result exactly. But 14 stations report more votes than voter signatures, or more signatures than people on the voter list. There were 22 such records in round 1. These could be recording errors or something else; the original records need checking.

2

High turnout does not come with a surge for the winner

Adding votes for a candidate can push up both turnout and that candidate’s share. I do not see that simple pattern here. I also checked stations above a high-turnout threshold taken from round 1. Even removing that whole group would raise Yotova’s share in the checked stations slightly, from 58.33% to 58.70%. That group is not a count of fake votes. Round 1 is useful for comparison, but it had unresolved findings too.

3

One pattern in Yotova’s vote counts needs explaining

The second digits of Yotova’s station vote counts differ from what the comparison model expects. The warning remains after allowing for the fact that I ran four digit checks. It is a reason to look closer, not proof that anyone invented votes. Station size, voting method, or a poor comparison model could also help explain it.

Where did the result change between rounds?

Kardzhali moves to Gyurov; Sofia-25 and Plovdiv-city move to Yotova.

Yotova leads in 28 of the 31 districts inside Bulgaria. Her largest leads are in Montana, Vratsa and Dobrich. Gyurov leads in Sofia-23, Sofia-24 and Kardzhali, as well as among voters abroad. Kardzhali changes most: Yotova’s 21.6-point lead in round 1 becomes a 19.0-point lead for Gyurov. Sofia-25 and Plovdiv-city change in Yotova’s favour. These shifts do not by themselves suggest fraud: voters who backed other candidates in round 1 had to choose again.

How to read the charts: district figures use stations with at least 100 registered voters and at least one voter signature, counted separately in each round. They keep the published numbers even where a station record does not balance. Leads are calculated per 100 signatures; the national result uses votes for the three ballot choices instead.

i
Turnout rose in all 31 domestic districts

More people voted in every district, though turnout remained low.

In the stations used for these charts, turnout rose from 21.43% to 26.71%. Kardzhali still had the lowest turnout, at 12.8%; Sofia-23 had the highest, at 43.7%. The chart on the right asks whether places where more people voted also gave Yotova a bigger lead. The link is weak and runs the other way: r = −0.29, where zero would mean no straight-line link. A link between places does not tell us why people voted as they did.

!
Do the station records add up?

The national totals match, but 14 station records contain conflicting numbers.

I took one complete counting record per station, including machine votes where present, and avoided counting the same votes twice. Adding the station results gives exactly the published total for each candidate and for “Do not support anyone”. But 14 records—12 inside Bulgaria and 2 abroad—show more votes than signatures, or more signatures than registered voters. The voter count includes people added to the list on election day. The records need checking to find out why the numbers conflict.

For the pattern checks below, I left out 613 stations abroad, 12 Bulgarian stations with conflicting records, and 1,436 stations with fewer than 100 registered voters. That leaves 10,656 stations, with 6,555,339 registered voters and 1,750,174 signatures. In this group, turnout is 26.70% and Yotova has 58.33% of the signature count. This differs from her national 57.566% because both the stations and what is being counted differ. Downloads: stations left out and why, selection totals, check against national totals.

Checks for unusual voting patterns

Each check looks for a different warning sign. Open one to see what it looks for, what I found, and how to read the chart. All use the same 10,656 selected stations inside Bulgaria. A warning means “look closer”, not “fraud proved”.

i
Extra votes in high-turnout stations

Removing the whole high-turnout group would slightly increase Yotova’s share.

Adding fake votes can make both turnout and a candidate’s total unusually high. This check, often called the Shpilkin method, looks at the stations where turnout is highest.

I used round 1 to set a dividing line at 50.48% turnout. Above it, there are 28,904 votes for Yotova—2.83% of her votes in the stations checked. If I removed the whole group, her share would rise from 58.33% to 58.70%. So this group does not explain her lead.

Read the chart: the shaded area marks high turnout. It does not mark fake votes. The dividing line depends on the comparison chosen, and round 1 has unresolved findings of its own.

i
More voters, more support for the winner?

Yotova does not gain a bigger share as turnout rises overall.

If votes are added for one candidate, stations with more voters can also show a bigger share for that candidate. On a dot chart, that can look like a rising tail—hence the name “comet” check.

Here, each dot is a station. Yotova’s support does not generally rise with turnout: the measured link is weak and negative (−0.151). For Gyurov it is weak and positive (+0.155). That does not show the simple added-votes pattern for Yotova.

Real differences between places can also create these shapes. This check cannot rule out other kinds of cheating.

i
Groups of stations with different voting patterns

The software finds two groups. That alone says nothing about fraud.

This check looks at turnout and Yotova’s share together. It tries to describe the results as two groups of stations. The technical name is a Klimek-style electoral fingerprint.

The two groups have average turnout of about 20.2% and 28.9%, and average Yotova shares of about 51.1% and 62.2%. The second group has a fitted weight of 0.751. That is a number in the grouping model, not an estimate that 75.1% of votes are fraudulent.

Different cities and rural areas could help explain the groups, but I have not tested that explanation. Finding two groups is not evidence that one of them cheated.

✓
Unusually round percentages

No unusual pile-up at whole numbers such as 60%, 70% or 75%.

People inventing results might favour tidy percentages. But honest counts can also produce whole numbers, especially in small stations. The Kobak check compares how often this happens with computer-made counts that allow for station size.

Whole-number turnout appears in 1.623% of checked stations, compared with 1.819% expected in the simulation. Yotova’s whole-number shares are also slightly less common than expected. Percentages divisible by five show no unusual pile-up either.

Read the chart: compare the actual bar with the computer comparison. Neither actual bar is higher. The score called z measures distance from the comparison; it is not a probability of fraud.

!
Unusual second digits in vote counts

Yotova’s counts have an unusual digit pattern that needs explaining.

Take a station count of 143 votes: its second digit is 4. I count these digits across stations, using results of at least 10 votes. A rule called Benford’s law gives one expected pattern. I also compare with computer-made counts shaped like the real ones, because Benford’s law alone may not suit election results.

Yotova has 1,452 second-digit zeros among 10,445 counts, compared with about 1,250 expected under Benford. Digits 0–3 are more common, and 5–9 less common, than that rule predicts. The warning remains after adjusting for running four digit checks (Holm-adjusted p = 0.0100). Gyurov does not trigger that warning.

What it means: the pattern is hard to explain under this comparison model. It is not a 1% chance that the election was honest, or a 99% chance of fraud. Station sizes, voting methods and a poor comparison model could also matter. The calculation uses 400 simulations, so its precision is limited.

✓
Unusual last digits in vote counts

Neither candidate’s last digits raise a warning after allowing for multiple checks.

In a count of 143 votes, the last digit is 3. Invented numbers can favour some endings over others. I check counts of at least 100 votes so that very small totals do not dominate the result.

I checked 5,089 counts for Yotova and 2,037 for Gyurov. Once I allow for having run four digit checks, neither candidate’s last digits are unusual enough to trigger a warning. The adjusted p-values are 0.1421 and 0.3890.

Passing this particular check does not mean every vote was counted honestly.

i
Stations that differ sharply from nearby stations

364 stations stand out. Their records are worth checking.

I compare a station’s turnout and Yotova’s share with other checked stations in the same municipality. A big difference can be worth investigating, even if the national totals look ordinary.

This check picks out 364 stations. In the chart, the dashed lines mark the rule used to pick them: a distance score above 4 or below −4 on either measure. The technical name is a robust z-score.

A station can stand out for an ordinary reason, such as serving a different community. These are places to check, not 364 proven cases of fraud.

A computer search for unusual stations

The program selects 107 stations for a closer look. It does not count fraud.

A program called Isolation Forest looks for unusual combinations: how many people voted, how much support Yotova received, station size and other recorded totals.

I set it to pick roughly the most unusual 1% of stations. It selects 107. That number largely follows from my setting—it does not mean 1% of stations cheated.

Read the chart: further right means more unusual according to the program. The dashed line marks the stations selected for review. This helps decide where to look first; it does not tell us what happened there.

What changed since round 1?

Turnout rose by about five people per 100 registered voters in the typical station.

I can compare 10,581 stations that passed the selection rules in both rounds. The middle station’s turnout rose by 5.28 percentage points; the average rise was 5.37. Turnout rose by more than 15 points in 215 stations and fell by more than 15 in 43. Those larger changes are worth a closer look.

The software also marks 199 stations where turnout and Yotova’s share both change sharply. But the share part is hard to interpret: there were 27 candidates in round 1 and only two in the runoff. Large gains are expected when other candidates drop out.

The left chart uses all selected stations in each round (10,616 and 10,656). The right chart compares only the same 10,581 stations. The high-turnout dividing line used earlier comes from the full round-1 group. Round 1 is a comparison, not a guarantee of honest results.

i
Trying the checks on deliberately changed data

The added-votes check reacts; the round-number check reacts only weakly.

To see whether the checks notice changes, I make experimental copies of the data. In one, I add made-up votes for Yotova. In a separate experiment, I push turnout toward round percentages. The real results are not changed.

After adding votes, Yotova’s count in the high-turnout group grows from 14,683 to 78,570—about 5.35 times as much. So that check reacts. In the rounding experiment, the distance score moves from −1.91 to +0.66: a modest change, not strong detection.

This exercise shows how the checks behave in these two made-up situations. It does not show whether real votes were fake. This experiment uses a turnout dividing line of 58.41%, while the main added-votes check uses 50.48%, so their starting totals differ.

These checks use published numbers. They cannot tell whether someone was pressured to vote, paid for their vote, or wrongly included on a voter list. I did not inspect physical ballots. Round 1 helps with comparisons, but its own results also have unresolved questions.

i
What these checks cannot tell us

Numbers can reveal warning signs. They cannot settle every question about an election.

  • I saved the completed published count, not a series of election-night updates. I cannot use it to check unusual changes during the count.
  • The pattern checks cover 10,656 selected stations in Bulgaria. They leave out stations abroad, very small voter lists and records with conflicting numbers. The size filter does not reliably separate ordinary stations from mobile or institutional stations.
  • A candidate gaining votes between rounds is normal: voters whose first choice lost must choose again. Those gains alone do not show fraud.
  • I did not check physical ballots, original paper records, voter-list accuracy, pressure on voters, vote buying or campaign conditions. These number checks cannot certify that the election was free and fair.
